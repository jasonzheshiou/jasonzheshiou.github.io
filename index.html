Study one · can a model read an experience study

Two in five of the model's flags were false alarms.

Before the benchmark existed, “an LLM can do experience analysis” was a vibe. After it there was a number with a denominator, and a price: every flag costs an analyst an hour whether it was real or not. The uncomfortable parts are on the record too — tuning scores ran 30 points optimistic against the held-out split, and the two models cost 3.4× different amounts for near-identical answers.

Study two · the same applicant, decided twice

The spread ran from nothing at all to 47 %, and it concentrated where the rules are ambiguous.

One synthetic case file, one underwriting agent, the same rules and documents each time. Setting temperature to zero changed which decision came back. The plumbing is deterministic — so the variance is judgement, not code. That is the useful split, and it is the one people usually conflate.

The method, written up →
Measurement

Generate the truth, never curate it

A curated answer key drifts and cannot be regenerated as the test grows. Both studies start from a deterministic generator and are scored by a scorer that counts false alarms.

Governance

Every claim ships the bytes behind it

Frozen harness snapshots, prompt hashes, decisions logged in JSONL, sealed manifests per scenario. A number without the instrument that produced it is an anecdote — mine included.

Accountability

Agents propose, a named human signs

Escalation rules, permissions and conservatism belong in the architecture, not in the prompt wording. Three roles debate above a deterministic rule engine; a person signs the outcome.

What open-sourcing it has meant

I started publishing for a dull reason: I wanted the work to be checkable.

A CV can say anything. A repository with a generator, a scorer and a log either supports the claim or it does not, and I would rather be argued with against evidence than admired against a summary.

It turned into something else. Everything here was built in broken time — forty minutes between a full-time job and childcare — which meant nothing could need me to sit and watch it. So I stopped writing code line by line and started designing loops I could delegate, then measuring what came out, because I could no longer inspect the models or my own code the way I used to. The same three-part discipline the profession needs: delegate the work, verify the behaviour, keep the sign-off. The constraint was the teacher.

It also made me a better actuary. Reading a benchmark result, a variance distribution or a vendor's model card is a different task once you have been the one who had to produce all three honestly.

Receipts over claims the habit came from being wrong in public, with the record proving which part was wrong failures are the useful part: a run that retried forever, a tool nobody used, two claims that died at their noise floor architecture over heroics: delegation, resumable runs, handoff documents

What this work does not say.

  • No real policyholder data appears anywhere. Every book is synthetic and seeded from one number.
  • No claim of accuracy beyond the sealed key — and the key is synthetic, so “false alarm” means nothing was planted there.
  • No production deployment of the agent engines, no regulatory sign-off, and no replacement for a signing actuary.
  • Lapse and expense A/E work is planned and deliberately untested, so it carries no number here.

Bring me a hard question.

The most useful message I can get is a reason why one of these results does not hold. Decision records live in the repositories' logs, so a disagreement can be argued against evidence rather than against a summary of it. Glad to talk with actuaries, engineers and regulators about the governance patterns.
