Study one · can a model read an experience study

Two in five of the model's flags were false alarms.

Before the benchmark existed, “an LLM can do experience analysis” was a vibe. After it there was a number with a denominator: of everything the harness flagged, 41.9 % pointed at nothing planted, with precision of 58.1 % against a sealed key. Best cell on a held-out split, 71.2 %. Every flag costs an analyst an hour, so that figure is not a score. It is a price.

The uncomfortable parts are on the record too: tuning scores ran 30 points optimistic against the held-out split, and the two models cost 3.4× different amounts to run for near-identical answers. Read the study.

Study two · the same applicant, decided twice

The spread ran from nothing at all to 47 %, and it concentrated where the rules are ambiguous.

One synthetic case file, one underwriting agent, the same rules and the same documents each time. The offered sum assured moved 0–47 % across repeat decisions: zero on the rule-based path, up to 47 % where the case is genuinely hard. Setting temperature to zero changed which decision came back. The plumbing is deterministic — 600 repeat runs of the suite, 0 divergence — so the variance is judgement, not code. That is the useful split, and it is the one people usually conflate.

Three agent roles sit in the loop — the analyst who reads the file, the challenger who argues the adverse case, and the engine that applies the fixed rules on top. A named actuary signs the outcome; the model never does. Read the study.

Method

Three things have to be true before an AI output can enter an insurance decision.

Measurement. Generate the truth; never curate it. A curated answer key drifts and cannot be regenerated as the test grows, so both studies start from a deterministic generator and are scored by a scorer that counts false alarms rather than rewarding the model for talking more.

Governance. Every claim ships the bytes behind it: frozen harness snapshots, prompt hashes, decisions logged in JSONL, sealed manifests per scenario. A number without the instrument that produced it is an anecdote — mine included.

Accountability. Agents propose; a named human decides. Escalation rules, permissions and conservatism belong in the architecture, not in the prompt wording. The full method is written up, including the part that is still only a design.

The projects, plainly

Six things, and why each one is here.

  1. Synthetic data generator Australian death, trauma, disability and income-protection experience: 250 000 policies from one seed, trends and shocks planted deliberately, answer key sealed. Nothing here is checkable without it.
  2. Experience bench Forty-seven sealed exams, two models crossed with two harnesses, a scorer that counts inventions. It turned confident vibes into numbers.
  3. Multi-agent underwriter Medical, financial and compliance agents debating above a deterministic rule engine, with a human signing on top. It shows where the human actually goes.
  4. Analysis dashboard · Standard Inquirer A trauma-incidence study with credibility weighting, trend tests and model selection, with an audit trail; and compliance answers that cite the standard rather than a model's recollection of it.
  5. Status watch · schema and Excel tooling A Raspberry Pi that tells my phone when the shared model server is free, plus schema inspection and join planning. Measurement needs a queue, and nothing downstream works if the joins are wrong.
  6. Pixel Storybook · little games Side projects with no commercial excuse, keeping the habits honest: pre-register the question, publish the noise floor, keep the artefacts. Two of three claims there failed their own noise floor, and that is on the record.

What open-sourcing it has meant

I started publishing for a dull reason: I wanted the work to be checkable.

A CV can say anything. A repository with a generator, a scorer and a log either supports the claim or it does not, and I would rather be argued with against evidence than admired against a summary.

It turned into something else. Everything here was built in broken time — forty minutes between a full-time job and childcare — which meant nothing could need me to sit and watch it. So I stopped writing code line by line and started designing loops I could delegate, then measuring what came out, because I could no longer inspect the models or my own code the way I used to. The same three-part discipline the profession needs: delegate the work, verify the behaviour, keep the sign-off. The constraint was the teacher.

It also made me a better actuary. Reading a benchmark result, a variance distribution or a vendor's model card is a different task once you have been the one who had to produce all three honestly.

The boundaries

What this work does not say.

  • No real policyholder data appears anywhere. Every book is synthetic and seeded from one number.
  • No claim of accuracy beyond the sealed key — and the key is synthetic, so “false alarm” means nothing was planted there.
  • No production deployment of the agent engines, no regulatory sign-off, and no replacement for a signing actuary.
  • Lapse and expense A/E work is planned and deliberately untested, so it carries no number here.
