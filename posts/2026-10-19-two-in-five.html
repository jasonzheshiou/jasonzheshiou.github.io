Two in five, and why that is the useful number

2026-10-19 · 2 min read

Draft

Not published yet: the figures and links are still being checked, so this page is left out of the feed and out of search.

Before the benchmark existed, "an LLM can do experience analysis" was a vibe. After it, there was a number with a denominator: 41.9 % of the flags were false alarms, with precision of 58.1 % against a sealed answer key.

That sentence is more useful than either half of the usual argument.

What was actually asked of the model

The benchmark is a synthetic book — 250 000 seeded lives across four benefit lines, with anomalies planted on purpose — and 47 scenarios built so the answer key never reaches the model. Two Qwen 27B models, crossed with two harnesses, asked to do the thing every experience analyst does: look at actual-over-expected and say where the story is.

A false alarm here is not "the model said something wrong". It is: the model flagged a cell, and the sealed record says nothing was planted there. Every flag costs a human an hour. That is the price the number is measuring.

Why not just report precision

Because precision without a denominator is a mood. 58.1 % on 47 scenarios is not the same claim as 58.1 % on 4 000, and the site says which one it is. The whole point of publishing the harness, the answer key's construction and the commit (8067f00) is that the number can be re-derived and argued with.

The 41.9 % is also the cheapest argument I have against the demo. A demo shows you one flag it got right. A benchmark tells you how many you would have had to sit through to get it.

What I would change

The key is synthetic, so "false alarm" is defined by what I chose to plant. A fresh held-out exam, drawn from a different seed family, is the obvious next step — and the reason the standing on that page says measured rather than proven.

The full write-up, including every figure and what each one excludes, is in the A/E benchmark.
